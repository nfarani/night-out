# Product brief

## Origin

Started as "pub roulette" — enter a postcode, roll a dice, get a random London
pub. Grew into multi-category itineraries (perfume shop → food → pub). Then hit
the question that reframed everything:

> People already have Google. Why would they use this?

That objection is correct for the original version and has driven every
subsequent change. It should keep being applied.

## The actual insight

Google Maps solves **search**. It gives you a list and makes you choose.
Choosing is the annoying part — the "where do you want to go" / "I don't mind,
you pick" loop that eats forty minutes of a group chat.

The product is a **decision tool**, not a discovery tool. Everything that
follows comes from that distinction.

## Why randomness is not enough

A dice roll removes the choice, which is genuinely different from search. But
novelty mechanics have poor retention — tried once, screenshotted, abandoned.
Randomness survives as a *detail inside* a real product, not as the product.

The stronger forms of the same insight:
- **Preference aggregation** — everyone taps a few things they'd be up for, the
  app proposes the overlap. Removes the person who has to be the organiser.
- **Commitment device** — "the app said The Ten Bells, we're going" is a
  different social script from "I found a place." People like outsourcing a
  decision they don't want to own.
- **Ritual** — a crawl, a route, a night out, not a venue lookup.

## Two hypotheses

### A — Consumer group-decision app
- Wedge: nobody has solved the group "where are we going" argument
- Retention: lives in the group, not the individual; switching costs once
  friends' preferences are in it
- Money: has to become transactional (commission on bookings) — discovery
  alone captures none of the value it creates
- Risk: consumer acquisition is expensive; group coordination apps are a
  graveyard

### B — B2B itinerary engine for publishers
- Wedge: publishers already produce curation; they have no way to turn a
  listicle into a plan a reader walks. Their content converts to a pageview
  and nothing else.
- Product: content-agnostic engine. Curated list in → sequenced, routed,
  shareable plan out, plus engagement analytics.
- Money: licensing / white-label, then possibly acquisition
- Risk: media companies underinvest in product and have thin budgets. They may
  not buy. The counter-argument is that their in-house tech capacity is thin
  and a working product with usage is cheaper than staffing a team.

They share roughly 80% of the engine. **Build shared primitives.**

## What makes it defensible

Not the data — Google Places is a commodity and anyone can call it. Building on
it alone recreates the "you're a worse Google" problem.

**Curation is the moat.** An opinionated set — "three places within eight
minutes that work in the rain," "the Sunday where nothing opens before eleven,"
"cheap enough that a second round isn't a decision" — is something Google
structurally cannot produce, because it optimises for the average query and
surfaces the most-reviewed, most-touristed option.

## Open questions

- Which hypothesis leads? They diverge after the shared engine.
- Does the reshuffle mechanic actually feel good, or just clever? Untested with
  real users.
- Would a publisher pay for engagement analytics on their own content, or do
  they not measure that deeply today?
- Is there a version where Nick curates the packs himself and runs it as a
  consumer product, skipping publishers entirely?
